**ASIN Confirmation Rate**

**Definition**

The ASIN confirmation rate attempts to account for vendor selection feedback and represents the number of units confirmed by vendors for a specific ASIN versus the number of units Amazon needed to fulfil customer demand.

**Average Cost per Unit**

**Definition**

Average Cost per Unit (ACU) refers to the average cost of goods sold for each unit shipped. It is calculated as SUM(PRODUCT_COGS_AMT) / SUM(QUANTITY_SHIPPED), returning 0 when QUANTITY_SHIPPED is zero.

**Example**

If a product has a total product cost of goods sold (PRODUCT_COGS_AMT) of $50,000 and a quantity shipped of 10,000 units, the ACU would be $5.00. This means that, on average, each shipped unit costs $5.00 in product cost of goods sold. You can use this metric to compare cost efficiency across products, vendors, or time periods and identify opportunities to reduce per-unit costs

**Why is it important?**

ACU shows how much it costs, on average, to produce or acquire each unit that is shipped. If ACU is high, it may indicate rising procurement or manufacturing costs, which could signal a need to renegotiate supplier terms, optimize sourcing strategies, or review product pricing. If ACU is low, it suggests efficient cost management per unit, but it’s important to ensure quality and margins are not being compromised.

**Confirmed Quantity**

**Definition**

Number of units confirmed to be dispatched to Amazon, out of the total number of units on purchase orders submitted by Amazon.

**Contra COGS**

**Definition**

Contra Cost of Goods Sold (CCOGS) refers to the adjustments to product costs that are recorded when items are dispatched to customers. It is calculated as the total cost adjustments applied when products move from inventory to customer delivery.

**Example**

If a product has a CCOGS of $10,000, it means $10,000 in cost adjustments were applied when these items shipped to customers. This helps you understand the true cost impact of your fulfilled orders and aids in pricing decisions.

**Why is it important?**

CCOGS helps measure cost adjustments in your delivery operations. If CCOGS is high, you have significant cost adjustments affecting your product margins when items are dispatched. If CCOGS is low, you either have fewer shipments or smaller cost adjustments on your dispatched items. This affects your overall profitability calculations.

**Also known as**

* VFCC

**Conversion rate**

**Definition**

Conversion rate refers to how likely customer buy something after looking at it. It’s how many units of a product people bought (i.e., Units Ordered) compared to the number of times people looked at it (i.e., Glance Views).

**Example**

If a product has a Conversion Rate of 5%, that means for every 100 customers who look at the product page, 5 of them will buy the product, after counting any orders that are canceled.

**Why is it important?**

Conversion Rate shows how good your product, the price and the details about the product are at getting customers to buy it. If the Conversion Rate is high, that means a lot of customers who look at your product end up buying it. If the Conversion Rate is low, that means you may need to change something about the product, the price or the information about it. Making those changes will help boost how likely customers are to purchase the product after they view the details.

**Customer Initiated Returned % (CIR)**

**Definition**

Customer Initiated Returned % (CIR) refers to the share of shipped items that buyers returned, or received a full refund or free replacement for without returning. It is calculated as total conceded units divided by total shipped units, including Returned Item Refunds, Unreturned Item Refunds and Intentional Return less Refunds, and excluding Outbound Items Not Received and Special Case Refunds.

**Example**

If a product has a Customer Initiated Returned % (CIR) of 5%, it means 5 of every 100 shipped items were returned or refunded without return.

**Why is it important?**

Customer Initiated Return % (CIR) shows the share of shipped items buyers returned or were refunded for. If Customer Initiated Return % (CIR) is high, that means you should review listing accuracy, sizing information and packaging. If Customer Initiated Return % (CIR) is low, that means your products are matching buyer expectations.

**Dispatched Revenue**

**Definition**

Shipped Revenue refer to the total gross revenue earned from customer purchases before any promotions or discounts. This will match the top-line amount (i.e. before promotions) that the customer sees in the cart. It is calculated by multiplying the listed selling price by the quantity of items dispatched to customers.

**Example**

If a product has a shipped revenue amount of $1,000, it means customers paid $1,000 in total for this item before any promotions or discounts were applied. For instance, if the listed price was $20 and 50 units were shipped, the revenue share amount would be $1,000 ($20 \_ 50 units).

**Why is it important?**

It helps track your gross sales performance. If the amount is high, it indicates strong customer demand at your listed prices. If the amount is low, you might need to review your pricing strategy or product visibility. This metric provides the foundation for analysing your overall sales

**Glance Views**

**Definition**

Glance Views is the number of times the offer was displayed on product detail pages and won the Featured Offer.

**Example**

If a product has 1,000 glance views, it means your offer was shown as the primary purchase option 1,000 times on that product’s detail page. This helps you understand how often customers see your offer first when viewing your products.

**Why is it important?**

Glance Views help track your product’s visibility to customers. If the number is high, it means your offers are frequently winning the primary purchase position, potentially leading to better sales conversion. If the number is low, other sellers might be winning the featured position more often, which could impact your sales performance.

**In-Transit Inventory Units (Sellable)**

**Definition**

Sellable in-transit inventory units refers to the total number of sellable units that are currently being transported between warehouses, fulfilment centres or other storage locations at the end of a time period.

**Example**

If a product has sellable in-transit inventory units of 1,000, it means 1,000 units are currently being moved between locations. This information helps you make better decisions about inventory replenishment and ensures that you maintain optimal stock levels across your network.

**Why is it important?**

Sellable in-transit inventory units helps monitor your supply chain movement. If the number is high, you may need to account for longer delivery times and adjust your reorder points. If the number is low, you either have efficient delivery systems or might need to check if you’re maintaining enough safety stock.

**MSRP**

**Definition**

MSRP stands for Manufacturer’s Suggested Retail Price. It’s the price that a product’s manufacturer recommends retailers charge customers for that product.

**Net Dispatched GMS**

**Definition**

Net Shipped GMS refers to the total monetary value of all products that have been successfully sold and shipped to customers. It is calculated as the sum of listed prices, shipping, taxes, and gift wrapping charges, micus the cost of returns and refunds. This metric provides insight into the overall sales performance value generated on the Amazon marketplace, focusing solely on shipped orders that were not subsequently refunded to the customer.

**Net ordered GMS**

**Definition**

Net ordered GMS (Gross Merchandise Sales) represents the total monetary value of all orders after all promotions, discounts and deals have been applied, excluding cancelled orders. It captures the actual amount that customers agree to pay at the checkout.

**Example**

If a product shows $500 in net ordered GMS, customers placed orders totalling $500 after all promotions were applied. This represents the actual revenue amount regardless of promotion type.

**Why is it important?**

Net ordered GMS provides actual sales performance across all types of promotions and time periods. Net ordered GMS reflects the amount customers pay, unlike sales from units ordered, which only records promotional discount for Price Discounts, not other promotion types.

**Net PPM**

**Definition**

Net Pure Profit Margin (Net PPM) refers to the total profit margin after accounting for both product sales and vendor funding adjustments. It is calculated as (Revenue - Cost Price + Vendor Funded Adjustments - Sales Discounts) divided by Revenue, expressed as a percentage.

**Example**

If a product has a Net PPM of 15%, it means you’re earning 15 cents in profit for every dollar of revenue, after considering all costs and vendor funding. This helps you evaluate product performance and identify opportunities to improve profitability through pricing adjustments, cost negotiations or vendor funding programmes.

**Why is it important?**

Net PPM helps measure the overall profitability of your products. If Net PPM is high, it indicates strong pricing power and effective vendor funding programmes. If Net PPM is low, you might need to review pricing strategies, cost structures or vendor funding arrangements. This metric helps balance revenue growth with profitability targets.

**Net Receipts**

**Definition**

Cost that Amazon received from vendors minus the cost that were returned to vendors. The cost returned to vendors take into account all ASINs that were returned due to damaged merchandise.

**Net Receipts (Units)**

**Definition**

Net Receipts is the total number of units Amazon received after deducting the amount returned to supplies during the selected time period.

**Example**

If you received 1,200 total units for a product but 200 units were returned to suppliers over the same period, you would net 1,000 units. This helps you track actual inventory growth and assess supplier quality, as high return rates might indicate problems with particular suppliers or products.

**Why is it important?**

Net receipts help track your true inventory intake. If net receipts are high, it indicates strong inventory growth with few quality issues requiring returns. If net receipts are low or negative, it could signal quality problems with received goods or overstock situations requiring returns to suppliers. This metric is crucial for accurate inventory

**On-Hand Inventory Cost (Unsellable)**

**Definition**

On-hand inventory cost (unsellable) refers to the total cost of damaged or unusable inventory stored in Amazon Fulfilment Centres at the end of a time period. It is calculated by multiplying the number of unsellable units by their unit cost.

**Example**

If a product category has an on-hand inventory cost (unsellable) of $10,000, it means you’re holding $10,000 worth of inventory that cannot be sold due to damage or quality issues. This helps you identify where to focus quality improvement efforts and assess the financial impact of inventory handling processes.

**Why is it important?**

On-hand inventory cost (unsellable) helps measure potential losses from damaged inventory. If the cost is high, you may need to investigate issues with storage conditions, handling procedures, or supplier quality. If the cost is low, it suggests effective inventory management and quality control processes. This metric directly impacts your profit margins and write-off expenses.

**Ordered revenue**

**Definition**

Sales from units ordered (Ordered revenue) is the total revenue generated from products that customers have purchased. It is calculated by multiplying the selling price by the number of units ordered.

**Example**

If a product has $500 in sales from units ordered (ordered revenue), that means the total revenue generated from the units sold of that product is $500.

**Why is it important?**

Sales from units ordered (Ordered revenue) are important as they provide insights into the overall sales performance of a business. If this number is high, it indicates that the products are in high demand and the business is generating significant revenue. A low number could suggest issues with product pricing, marketing or customer satisfaction. Sales from units ordered is similar to Ordered Product Sales in Business Reports, but they handle cancelled orders differently. Business Reports tracks cancellations based on the order date, while Custom Analytics tracks cancellations based on the cancellation date. This difference in tracking cancellations can cause minor variations in the data, depending on when orders are cancelled.

**Also known as**

* Sales from units ordered

**Ordered units**

**Definition**

Ordered units is the total number of products that customers have purchased. It counts all items purchased, regardless of whether they have been dispatched.

**Example**

If a product has 50 units ordered, that means customers bought 50 units of that product.

**Why is it important?**

Ordered units are important as they show the number of products customers have purchased. If this number is high, it means that your products are popular and you’re meeting customer demand. A low number may indicate a need to adjust your product offerings, pricing or marketing to drive more sales. The Units Ordered metric is similar to the one in Business Reports, but it handles cancelled orders differently. Business Reports tracks cancellations based on the order date, while Custom Analytics tracks cancellations based on the cancellation date. This difference in tracking cancellations can cause minor variations in the data, depending on when orders are cancelled.

**Rebates**

**Definition**

Rebates refers to the COOP chargeback amount billed to the vendor for business discount programmes. It is calculated as the sum of coop_amount from coop_csi_calculation_results for finalised chargebacks matched to contra-COGS transactions.

**Example**

If a product has rebates of $500, it means the vendor was billed $500 in COOP chargebacks for business discounts on that product during the period.

**Why is it important?**

Rebates shows the total amount billed to vendors for business discount programmes. If rebates are high, it means vendors are funding significant discounts for B2B customers. If rebates are low, it may indicate fewer vendor-funded discounts or lower B2B order volume.

**Sales Discount**

**Definition**

The actual expense incurred for promotional activities on dispatched items. It is calculated by summing up the costs of various promotional types, including gift cards, buy-x-get-y (BXGY) offers, employee discounts and other customer incentives.

**Example**

If a product has a sales promotion cost of $5 per unit, it means you’re spending $5 on average in promotional expenses for each unit sold. This helps you decide if you should adjust your promotion strategy or pricing to maintain profitable margins while staying competitive.

**Why is it important?**

It helps measure your marketing investment per sale. If the cost is high, you should check if your promotions are generating enough additional sales to justify the expense. If it’s low, you might be missing opportunities to attract more customers through promotional offers.
